\date{26 September 2026 \quad (preprint, not yet refereed)}

\begin{abstract}
For a tree $T$ with Laplacian $L(T)$, Brualdi and Goldwasser (1984) introduced the
\emph{Laplacian ratio} $\pi(T)=\per L(T)/\prod_v\deg v$ and asked which tree on $n$ vertices
maximizes it. We answer the question for every $n$. For every $n\ge 4$ the maximum is attained by a
\emph{spider of cherry arms}: a centre carrying pendant paths of length two (cherries) and
\emph{arms}, an arm being a vertex that carries cherries. For $n\ge 492$ the centre carries only arms,
almost all with five cherries, and the exceptional arms are fixed by $6(n-1)\bmod 11$ through an explicit
rule; for $4\le n\le 491$ the maximizer is given by an explicit table. Along the way we determine the
exact exponential growth rate of the maximum,
$\tfrac{64}{621}\rho^{n}\le\max_{|T|=n}\pi(T)\le 2\rho^{n-1}$ with $\rho=(621/64)^{1/11}$, and prove the
sharp inequality behind it: every pendant subtree $b$ has matching weight at most $\rho^{|b|}$, with
equality exactly for the five-cherry arm. The whole argument is formalized in Lean~4 with Mathlib and
checked by the Lean kernel, depending only on Lean's three standard axioms. Large parts of it are
certificates: exact data produced by untrusted generators and checked by the kernel. We explain the
mathematics, show where each piece lives in the formal development and why it is organized that way,
and discuss what the answer and the method suggest.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%=====================================================================================================

\subsection{The question}

Let $T$ be a tree on $n\ge2$ vertices, $D$ its diagonal degree matrix, $A$ its adjacency matrix and
$L(T)=D-A$ its Laplacian. Determinants and spectra of Laplacians are classical (see
Merris's survey~\cite{Merris1994}): by the matrix-tree theorem the principal minors count spanning trees. Permanents are a different matter. The permanent
$\per M=\sum_{\sigma}\prod_iM_{i,\sigma(i)}$ looks like the determinant without the signs, but it is
\#P-hard to compute in general~\cite{Valiant1979}, and there is no comparable structure theory.

Brualdi and Goldwasser~\cite{BG1984} studied $\per L(T)$ for trees and bipartite graphs. Since the
permanent of a Laplacian grows with the degrees, they normalized it and introduced the
\emph{Laplacian ratio}
\[
  \pi(T)=\frac{\per L(T)}{\prod_{v\in V(T)}\deg v}.
\]
They showed that $\pi(T)\ge 2$ for every tree on at least two vertices, with equality exactly for the
star, and they asked the natural companion question: \emph{which tree on $n$ vertices maximizes $\pi$?}

\subsection{A short history}

\paragraph{1984: the question.} Merris, Rebman and Watkins~\cite{MRW1981} had introduced the
permanental polynomial of the Laplacian a few years earlier; Brualdi and Goldwasser~\cite{BG1984} then
studied $\per L$ of trees and bipartite graphs systematically, giving bounds in terms of the number of
vertices, the degree sequence, the diameter and the matching number. Among much else, they showed that over trees on $n$ vertices the permanent
$\per L(T)$ itself is smallest for the star and largest for the path, so the permanent alone is not
interesting to optimize: it mostly rewards long paths with many vertices of degree two. Dividing by
$\prod_v\deg v$ removes that trivial effect, and they introduced the ratio $\pi(T)$ for exactly this
purpose. They proved the lower bound $\pi\ge2$ and posed extremal problems for the ratio, including
bounds for trees with a given bipartition and the maximum over all trees on $n$ vertices. Later work
sharpened the permanental bounds themselves; for instance, Geng, Hu and Li~\cite{GHL2010} determined the
minimal permanents of Laplacians of trees, also under a maximum-degree constraint.

\paragraph{2024--2025: the minimum side, and a conjecture for the maximum.} The minimum-type questions
were resolved first. Wu, Dong, Lai and Zeng~\cite{Wu2024} determined the minimum of $\pi$ over trees on $n$ vertices with
diameter at least $k$, and Wu, Dong and Lai~\cite{WDL2025} the minimum over trees with a given
bipartition. For the maximum, Wu, Dong and Lai made a precise conjecture. For odd $n$ the maximizer should
be the subdivided star with $(n-1)/2$ legs of length two, that is, a centre carrying only cherries,
$C^{(n-1)/2}$ in the notation below, with value
\[
  \pi\big(C^{(n-1)/2}\big)=2\Big(\frac32\Big)^{(n-3)/2};
\]
for even $n$ they conjectured explicit subdivided stars with closed-form values in the two classes
$n\equiv0$ and $n\equiv2\pmod4$. The conjecture is natural: cherries are the best small building block
(a cherry has matching weight $\tfrac32$ on two vertices), and for small $n$ it is correct.

\paragraph{2026: counterexamples.} Pant~\cite{Pant2026} refuted the conjecture. His trees are
caterpillars $T(a_1,\dots,a_m)$: a path $x_1x_2\cdots x_m$ in which $x_i$ carries $a_i$ cherries. He gave
three infinite families, $A_t=T(3,t,3)$ on $2t+15$ vertices, $B_t=T(t,t,t,t)$ on $8t+4$ vertices and
$C_t=T(t,t,t+1,t)$ on $8t+6$ vertices, whose ratios exceed the conjectured values from $t=4$ on, that is
from $n=23$, $36$ and $38$ respectively, one family for each residue class of the conjecture. He also
observed that the equality statement already fails at $n=21$, where $T(3,3,3)$ attains the conjectured
value with a different degree sequence. He concluded that determining the maximum remains open, and he
proposed no replacement.

\paragraph{How the pieces fit.} With the complete answer in hand (\cref{thm:main}), the earlier work
lines up exactly, which we find a pleasing check on everything involved:
\begin{itemize}
\item The Wu--Dong--Lai formulas give the true maximum for every $n\le22$, and for every even $n\le34$.
  They first fail at $n=23$, $36$ and $38$, precisely the sizes of Pant's first counterexamples $A_4$,
  $B_4$ and $C_4$.
\item At $n=21$ exactly two trees are maximal: the conjectured $C^{10}$ and Pant's $T(3,3,3)$. Rooted at
  its middle vertex, $T(3,3,3)$ is the spider $C^3A_3^2$ (three cherries and two arms with three cherries
  each), and the two values agree: both equal $19683/256$.
\item At $n=23$ Pant's $A_4=T(3,4,3)$ is \emph{the} maximizer: rooted at its middle vertex it is the
  spider $C^4A_3^2$. His other two families are caterpillars with four hubs, which are never spiders, so by
  \cref{thm:reduction} they are never maximal; at $n=36$ and $38$ the maximizers are $C^4A_4^3$ and
  $C^5A_4^3$.
\end{itemize}
So the answer grows out of both earlier proposals. Wu, Dong and Lai were right that pendant paths of
length two are the basic unit; Pant was right that grouping them on several hubs beats a single hub of
cherries. What neither family captures is the eventual structure: one hub carrying \emph{arms}, each a
second-level hub with exactly five cherries.

\subsection{The answer}

This paper answers the question for every $n$. The answer is built from two small pieces (\cref{fig:spider}).

\begin{definition}[cherries, arms, spiders]
A \emph{cherry} is a pendant path of length two. For $j\ge1$ an \emph{arm} $A_j$ is a vertex, adjacent
to the centre, that carries $j$ cherries and nothing else; it has $2j+1$ vertices. We write $A_0$ for
a leaf adjacent to the centre. A \emph{spider of cherry arms} (in this paper, simply a \emph{spider})
is a tree with a vertex, the \emph{centre}, all of whose branches are leaves, cherries or arms. We write
$C^{c}A_{j_1}A_{j_2}\cdots$ for the spider whose centre carries $c$ cherries and arms
$A_{j_1},A_{j_2},\dots$, and use exponents for repeated arms.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[main theorem]\label{thm:main}
For every $n\ge4$ the tree $\mathrm{bgMax}(n)$ below has $n$ vertices and maximizes $\pi$ over all trees
on $n$ vertices.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For $n\ge 492$, put $s=6(n-1)\bmod 11$. The centre of $\mathrm{bgMax}(n)$ carries only arms, and
  all of them are $A_5$ except:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item $s\in\{1,2,3,4\}$: $s$ arms are $A_6$; except that for $s=3$ with $n\le722$ and for $s=4$ with
    $n\le 2319$, instead $11-s$ arms are $A_4$;
  \item $s\in\{5,\dots,10\}$: $11-s$ arms are $A_4$.
  \end{itemize}
\item For $4\le n\le 491$, $\mathrm{bgMax}(n)$ is the spider listed in \cref{app:table}.
\end{enumerate}
For $n\le3$ there is only one tree on $n$ vertices.
\end{theorem}

The recipe is easier to remember than it looks. An $A_5$ has 11 vertices, an $A_4$ has 9 and an $A_6$
has 13. After the centre there are $n-1$ vertices to distribute, and $n-1\equiv2s\pmod{11}$. Each $A_6$
used in place of an $A_5$ adds $2$ modulo 11 and each $A_4$ subtracts $2$, so $s$ copies of $A_6$, or
$11-s$ copies of $A_4$, exactly absorb the remainder (\cref{tab:rule}). The theorem says which of the two
ways wins. The two late switches at $n=722$ and $n=2319$ are genuine: they are where two very close
competitors trade places (\cref{fig:race}).

\paragraph{Why five cherries.} The number $\rho=(621/64)^{1/11}\approx1.22948$ is the exact exponential
growth rate of the maximum.

\begin{theorem}[growth rate]\label{thm:growth}
For every $n\ge1$,
$\displaystyle \frac{64}{621}\,\rho^{n}\ \le\ \max_{|T|=n}\pi(T)\ \le\ 2\,\rho^{\,n-1}.$
\end{theorem}

The upper bound comes from a sharp inequality for pendant subtrees (\cref{thm:sharp}): every pendant
subtree $b$ contributes a factor at most $\rho^{|b|}$, with equality only for the five-cherry arm. So
each vertex of a tree can contribute at most a factor $\rho$ to $\pi$, and only vertices inside
five-cherry arms achieve it. A maximizer therefore uses as many five-cherry arms as the arithmetic of $n$
allows, and all the difficulty is in the lower-order terms. For comparison, Pant's families grow like
$(3/2)^{n/2}\approx1.2247^{n}$, just below $\rho$.

\subsection{How the proof goes}

The proof has three layers.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \emph{Algebra.} For a tree, the permanent of the Laplacian is a weighted count of matchings, and
  a cavity recursion computes it from the leaves up (\cref{sec:matching,sec:cavity}).
\item \emph{Reduction to spiders.} Rooting a tree at a vertex of maximum degree, we show that any tree
  which is not a spider is strictly beaten by some spider with the same number of vertices
  (\cref{sec:reduction}). The engine is the sharp inequality of \cref{sec:ceiling}, refined into per-vertex
  inequalities that measure how much each non-spider feature costs.
\item \emph{Optimization over spiders.} Spiders form an explicit family with a closed-form value. We
  solve the optimization over it exactly (\cref{sec:spiders}).
\end{enumerate}

Each layer is split by size, because different arguments are efficient in different ranges
(\cref{fig:map}):

\section{From permanents to matchings}\label{sec:matching}
%=====================================================================================================

The first step is classical, and it is what makes the problem tractable at all: for a tree, the
permanent of the Laplacian collapses to a sum over matchings.

\begin{theorem}[\cite{BG1984,Pant2026}]\label{thm:matchsum}
For a tree $T$,
\[
  \per L(T)=\sum_{M}\ \prod_{v\notin V(M)}\deg v,
  \qquad\text{hence}\qquad
  \pi(T)=\sum_{M}\ \prod_{uv\in M}\frac{1}{\deg u\,\deg v},
\]
the sums running over all matchings $M$ of $T$, including the empty one, and $V(M)$ denoting the set of
matched vertices.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
A term $\prod_i L_{i,\sigma(i)}$ of the permanent is nonzero only if every $i$ is fixed by $\sigma$ or
sent to a neighbour. The non-trivial cycles of $\sigma$ then run along edges of $T$. Since $T$ has no
cycles, they are transpositions along edges, that is, a matching $M$. A fixed point contributes
$L_{ii}=\deg i$ and a matched edge contributes $L_{ij}L_{ji}=(-1)(-1)=1$. Dividing by $\prod_v\deg v$
gives the second formula.
\end{proof}

So $\pi(T)$ is a \emph{weighted matching count}: every matching contributes the product, over its edges,
of $1/(\deg u\deg v)$. The empty matching contributes $1$; single edges contribute a little each; larger
matchings contribute products of these small numbers (\cref{fig:matching}). In statistical-physics
language, $\pi$ is the partition function of a monomer-dimer system in the sense of Heilmann and
Lieb~\cite{HL1972}, with a dimer on edge $uv$ carrying activity $1/(\deg u\deg v)$.

Two consequences are worth drawing out. First, $\per L(T)$ is not hard to compute for trees:
\cref{thm:matchsum} and the recursion of the next section compute it in linear time. Every value in this
paper is an exact rational that anyone can recompute. Second, the maximization problem now has a clear
tension. A matched edge is worth more when its endpoints have small degree, but small degrees mean few
edges, and every edge incident to a vertex raises that vertex's degree and so lowers the weight of all
its other edges. The maximizer resolves this tension in a surprisingly rigid way.

\begin{inlean}
This is \lean{pi\_eq\_msum} in \lean{Matching.lean}, proved for any finite acyclic simple graph with
Mathlib's \lean{Matrix.permanent}. It is the only place where the permanent enters the formal proof.
Everything afterwards works with the matching sum, called \lean{Aobj}, and the bridge theorem
\lean{pi\_utree} in \lean{R47Tree.lean} says that for the trees of the formalization \lean{Aobj t}
equals $\per L/\prod\deg$ of the realized graph. That is how the final theorem can be stated literally
for the permanent (\cref{sec:lean}).
\end{inlean}

%=====================================================================================================
\section{The cavity recursion}\label{sec:cavity}
%=====================================================================================================

Matching sums on trees can be computed from the leaves upward. This is the ``cavity method'' of
statistical physics (see, e.g., Mézard and Montanari~\cite{MM2009}); on a tree it is exact, and it is the
workhorse of the whole proof.

\paragraph{Planted branches.} A \emph{planted branch} $b$ is a rooted tree whose root will be attached to
one further vertex, its parent. Degrees are always measured in the final tree, so the root of $b$ has
degree $d_b=(\text{number of children})+1$. Let $T_b$ be the matching sum of \cref{thm:matchsum}
restricted to edges of $b$, and $T^{\circ}_b$ the same sum over matchings that leave the root of $b$
unmatched. The ratio
\[
  y_b=\frac{T^{\circ}_b}{d_b\,T_b}
\]
is the \emph{message} that $b$ sends to its parent: it measures how available the root of $b$ is to be
matched upward, per unit of its degree. Splitting the matchings of $b$ according to whether, and to
which child, the root is matched gives the recursion
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cavity}
  T_b=\Big(\prod_{c}T_c\Big)\frac{d_b+R_b}{d_b},\qquad
  y_b=\frac{1}{d_b+R_b},\qquad R_b=\sum_c y_c,
\end{equation}
where the products and sums run over the children $c$ of the root (\cref{fig:cavity}). Indeed
$T^{\circ}_b=\prod_cT_c$, and matching the root to child $c$ contributes
$\frac{1}{d_bd_c}T^{\circ}_c\prod_{c'\neq c}T_{c'}=\frac{y_c}{d_b}\prod_{c'}T_{c'}$. A leaf has $T=1$
and $y=1$. For a tree rooted at a vertex with $k$ children, the same computation with $d=k$ (the root has
no parent) gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:root}
  \pi(T)=\Big(\prod_{c}T_c\Big)\Big(1+\frac{R}{k}\Big),\qquad R=\sum_c y_c .
\end{equation}
Since \eqref{eq:cavity} multiplies in one factor per vertex, a useful rewriting is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logsum}
  \log T_b=\sum_{v\in b}\log\Big(1+\frac{R_v}{d_v}\Big).
\end{equation}

\section{The sharp ceiling and the growth rate}\label{sec:ceiling}
%=====================================================================================================

Write $\Fs=\tfrac1{11}\log\tfrac{621}{64}=\log\rho$ and, for a planted branch $b$ with $|b|$ vertices,
\[
  \bell(b)=\log T_b-|b|\,\Fs .
\]
Think of $\bell(b)$ as the \emph{profit} of the branch: its log-weight minus the best possible
log-weight for its size. The central inequality says profit is never positive.

\begin{theorem}[sharp ceiling]\label{thm:sharp}
Every planted branch $b$ satisfies $T_b\le\rho^{|b|}$, that is $\bell(b)\le0$, with equality if and only
if $b$ is the five-cherry arm $A_5$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Subactions: paying for a vertex with its children's credit}

By \eqref{eq:logsum}, the profit is a sum of local terms,
\[
  \bell(b)=\sum_{v\in b}e_v,\qquad e_v=\log\Big(1+\frac{R_v}{d_v}\Big)-\Fs .
\]
Some vertices have $e_v>0$ (a vertex whose children send it large messages does well locally) and some
have $e_v<0$ (a leaf has $e=-\Fs$). A term-by-term bound is hopeless. Instead we look for a
\emph{potential} $\varrho\ge0$ on vertices, depending only on a vertex's local state, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sub}
  e_v+\varrho(v)\ \le\ \sum_{c\text{ child of }v}\varrho(c)\qquad\text{for every vertex }v.
\end{equation}
Read \eqref{eq:sub} as a budget: each vertex may run a local profit $e_v$, but must pay for it out of
the credit $\varrho(c)$ banked by its children, and must also bank credit $\varrho(v)$ for its own
parent. Summing \eqref{eq:sub} over all vertices of $b$, each $\varrho(v)$ appears once on the left and
once on the right (at its parent), except the root's, so everything telescopes (\cref{fig:telescope}):
\[
  \bell(b)=\sum_ve_v\ \le\ -\varrho(\mathrm{root})\ \le\ 0 .
\]
In the language of ergodic optimization such a $\varrho$ is a \emph{subaction}~\cite{Bousch2001,Jenkinson2019}; its
existence is the discrete analogue of finding a calibrating potential for an optimal orbit.

\paragraph{The witness.} Remarkably, a very simple potential works. Writing $y$ for a vertex's message
and grouping vertices by their number of children,
\[
  \varrho_{\mathrm{wit}}=
  \begin{cases}
    \Fs & \text{leaf},\\[1pt]
    2\Fs-\log\tfrac32+\tfrac14\big(y-\tfrac13\big) & \text{one child},\\[1pt]
    \tfrac1{32}\,y & \text{two children},\\[1pt]
    \tfrac1{384}\,y & \text{three children},\\[1pt]
    0 & \text{four or more children}.
  \end{cases}
\]
Three instances of \eqref{eq:sub} are equalities, and they are exactly the three kinds of vertex in
$A_5$:
\begin{itemize}
\item a leaf: $e=-\Fs$ and $\varrho=\Fs$, so $e+\varrho=0$;
\item the middle vertex of a cherry (one leaf child, message $1/3$):
  $e+\varrho=(\log\tfrac32-\Fs)+(2\Fs-\log\tfrac32)=\Fs=\varrho(\text{leaf})$;
\item the arm vertex with five cherries ($d=6$, $R=5/3$, $\varrho=0$):
  $e=\log\tfrac{23}{18}-\Fs$, and the children's credit is $5(2\Fs-\log\tfrac32)$. These agree exactly
  when $11\Fs=\log\big(\tfrac{23}{18}(\tfrac32)^5\big)=\log\tfrac{621}{64}$, which is the definition of
  $\Fs$.
\end{itemize}
Every other instance of \eqref{eq:sub} holds with room to spare. Checking that is a finite computation
once one notices that \eqref{eq:sub} only involves a vertex and its children's local states, and that
large degrees are easy (the term $\log(1+R/d)$ is bounded, while the credit of the children grows with
their number). The verification splits into cells by degree and by ranges of the children's messages;
in each cell the inequality compares a rational envelope against a rational enclosure of a logarithm.

\paragraph{Equality.} If $\bell(b)=0$ then every inequality along $b$ is tight. Tightness at the root
forces degree six, and at degree six it forces all five children to be cherries: a single non-cherry
child makes the cell strict. The strictness needs the numerical fact $2\Fs-\log\tfrac32<\tfrac1{96}$,
which follows from $\log x<x-1$ at $x=(621/64)^2(2/3)^{11}\neq1$. At degree three the non-strict
version of this bound is exactly tight, so it cannot be weakened.

\subsection{The growth rate}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:growth}]
Root $T$ anywhere. In \eqref{eq:root} each child $c$ is a planted branch, so $\prod_cT_c\le\rho^{n-1}$
by \cref{thm:sharp}; and each $y_c\le1$, so $1+R/k\le2$. For the lower bound, take a centre
carrying $K=\lfloor (n-1)/11\rfloor$ arms $A_5$, and fill the remaining at most ten vertices with
cherries and at most one leaf; each has $T\ge1$, so $\pi\ge\rho^{11K}\ge\tfrac{64}{621}\rho^{n}$.
\end{proof}

\section{Every maximizer is a spider}\label{sec:reduction}
%=====================================================================================================

We now show that a tree that is not a spider cannot be a maximizer. The idea is to measure precisely
how much each departure from spider shape costs, and to show the cost always exceeds any possible gain.

\subsection{Setting up the comparison}

\paragraph{Rooting at maximum degree.} Rerooting a tree changes neither $\pi$ nor the vertex count, so
we may root at a vertex of maximum degree $k$. Then every vertex has at most $k-1$ children. This cap is
what makes finitely many local inequalities sufficient for each $k$. Call the root's children
$b_1,\dots,b_k$. The \emph{atoms} are the leaf, the cherry and the arms $A_j$; the tree is a spider
centred at the root exactly when every $b_i$ is an atom (\cref{fig:reduction}).

\paragraph{The profit identity.} Taking logarithms in \eqref{eq:root},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lagrange}
  \log\pi-(n-1)\Fs=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bell(b_i)+\log\Big(1+\frac{S}{k}\Big),\qquad S=\sum_i y_{b_i}.
\end{equation}
By \cref{thm:sharp} every term of the sum is $\le0$, so a tree's total profit is its root bonus
$\log(1+S/k)$ minus the losses of its branches. A non-atom branch has a strictly negative profit, but it
might send a larger message and so raise the root bonus. We must show that trade never pays.

\paragraph{The benchmark.} We compare against explicit spiders. The $A_5/A_4$ spider, with
$q\equiv5(n-1)\pmod{11}$ arms $A_4$ and the rest $A_5$, satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:floor}
  \log\pi-(n-1)\Fs\ \ge\ \log\tfrac{26}{23}-\tfrac{10}{960},
\end{equation}
because each $A_4$ costs little profit and sends a larger message than an $A_5$
($y(A_4)=\tfrac3{19}>\tfrac3{23}=y(A_5)$).

The argument then splits by the root degree $k$ and by $n$.

\subsection{High degree: $k\ge24$}

When the root has many children, the root bonus $\log(1+S/k)$ is nearly linear in $S$ over the relevant
range. Replace it by its tangent line at the spider value $S/k=3/23$ (\cref{fig:tangent}). The slope
prices each unit of message at $\mu=\tfrac{23}{26k}\le\tfrac{23}{624}$, and the profit identity becomes
a sum over branches of
\[
  V_\mu(b)=\bell(b)+\mu\,y_b,
\]
a branch's profit plus the market value of its message. On this range of prices we show that $A_5$
maximizes $V_\mu$ over all branches, and that every non-atom lies below it by at least $\tfrac1{75}$.
(The closest non-atom competitor, at $0.01452$ below, is a vertex carrying five cherries and one $A_4$.)
So a tree with a non-atom branch has $\log\pi-(n-1)\Fs\le\log\tfrac{26}{23}-\tfrac1{75}$, which is below
the benchmark \eqref{eq:floor} because $\tfrac1{75}>\tfrac{10}{960}$.

\subsection{Low degree and large $n$: linear deficits}

When $k\le23$ the tangent trick is too crude, and we use size instead. A tree with a non-atom branch
should lose profit in proportion to its size, while the root bonus is bounded. We strengthen the
subaction inequality \eqref{eq:sub} by a small fixed rate $\alpha$ per vertex. A \emph{rate cell} with
degree cap $D$ and rate $\alpha$ is a per-vertex inequality which, telescoped exactly as in
\cref{fig:telescope}, yields a \emph{linear deficit}
\[
  \bell(b)+\varrho_{\mathrm{wit}}(b)\ \le\ -\alpha|b|\qquad\text{for every non-atom branch }b
  \text{ with at most }D-1\text{ children per vertex.}
\]
Atoms are exempt, because $A_5$ has zero profit and cannot lose anything. Then
\[
  \log\pi-(n-1)\Fs\ \le\ R_k-\alpha(n-1),
\]
where $R_k$ bounds what the root can gain. With $R_k\le0.319$ and the rates below, this falls under the
benchmark once $n-1\ge491$. That is where the threshold $492$ comes from.

\subsection{Low degree, $150\le n\le491$: rate cells with credits}

Below $492$ the linear deficit is not yet large enough, and we sharpen it in two ways. First, in a tree
of maximum degree $\le D$ every message satisfies $y\ge 1/(2D-1)$, which narrows the range of messages
each cell must handle. Second, non-atom branches carry a \emph{credit} $\kappa$ depending on their
number of children, $\bell(b)+\varrho_{\mathrm{wit}}(b)\le-\alpha|b|-\kappa(b)$, which they pass up to
the root. The root is then bounded by an explicit knapsack over atoms, certified separately for each
pair $(k,n)$.

\begin{inlean}
\lean{BGSpiderMid.lean} proves the generic credited telescoping. The generated files
\lean{BGSpiderMidCellsD2}--\lean{D23} (cells, one file per cap) and \lean{BGSpiderMidRootK2}--\lean{K23}
(root knapsacks, one file per root degree) are the certificates. \lean{BGSpiderMidFinal.lean} assembles
\lean{spider\_dominates\_of\_maxDegreeRoot\_150}, and \lean{BGMax.maximizer\_is\_spider\_150} states the
result for every $n\ge150$.
\end{inlean}

\subsection{Low degree, $7\le n\le149$: the envelope certificate}

For small trees a uniform rate is far too weak, so we compute instead. The object computed is a
\emph{Bellman envelope}: a table $w(m,g)$ that bounds, for every branch of size $m$, its profit plus the
value of its message at price $\mu_g$, where the prices run over a grid (\cref{fig:envelope}).

Fix a rational rate $f$ close to $\Fs$ (it cancels between a tree and a spider of the same size), put
$V_{\mu}(b)=\log T_b-|b|f+\mu y_b$, and consider a vertex with its children. The function
$x\mapsto\log x+\mu/x$ is concave on the relevant range, so its tangent bounds $V_\mu$ at the vertex by
a constant plus $\sum_cV_{\nu}(c)$ at a new price $\nu$. In words: a vertex's value at price $\mu$ is
controlled by its children's values at a price that the vertex determines. The children's sizes add up
to $m-1$, so the best total over children is a max-plus knapsack over sizes. A table that satisfies all
these knapsack inequalities is a valid envelope by induction on size, and the proof needs only that
finitely many table inequalities hold.

\subsection{The smallest trees, and the summary}

For $4\le n\le6$ there are few enough trees to evaluate every one exactly over $\mathbb{Q}$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:reduction}
For every $n\ge7$, every tree on $n$ vertices that maximizes $\pi$ is, after rerooting at a vertex of
maximum degree, a spider. For $n\ge492$ every tree on $n$ vertices is weakly beaten by a spider on $n$
vertices.
\end{theorem}

\end{inlean}

%=====================================================================================================
\section{The best spider}\label{sec:spiders}
%=====================================================================================================

We now optimize over spiders. By \eqref{eq:root} and the table of atoms, a spider's value has a closed
form, and the problem becomes a question about integers.

\subsection{The closed form and the balance identity}

A spider is described up to isomorphism by the multiset of its centre's children, and
\[
  F=\Big(\frac32\Big)^{c+\sum j}\prod_{\text{arms}}\alpha_j\cdot\Big(1+\frac{c/3+\sum b_j}{D}\Big),
  \qquad \alpha_j=\frac{4j+3}{3(j+1)},\quad b_j=\frac{3}{4j+3},
\]
where $c$ is the number of cherries at the centre, the product and sum run over the arms (leaves being
$A_0$, with $\alpha_0=b_0=1$), and $D$ is the degree of the centre. The first factor is fixed by $n$ up
to the number of arms, so the optimization is a balance between the arm factors $\alpha_j$ and the
bracket.

\begin{lemma}[balance]\label{lem:balance}
Put $Q_c(x,y)=\alpha_x\alpha_y(c+b_x+b_y)$. For integers $k,t\ge0$,
\[
  Q_c(k+t+1,k+1)-Q_c(k+t+2,k)=\frac{(t+1)\big((c-3)(8k+4t+15)+3\big)}{9(k+1)(k+2)(k+t+2)(k+t+3)}.
\]
Consequently, if $D\ge3$, moving a cherry from an arm to an arm at least two smaller strictly increases
$F$, and in every maximizer with $D\ge3$ all arm sizes (leaves included) differ by at most one.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The move (\cref{fig:balance}) keeps $n$, $D$ and the power of $\tfrac32$, and multiplies $F$ by
$Q_c(\text{new})/Q_c(\text{old})$ with $c=D+R_0\ge3$, where $R_0$ is the message sum of the other
children. The right side of the identity is then positive. The identity itself is a polynomial
identity in $k,t,c$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Sizes up to 491: a kernel sweep}

By \cref{lem:balance}, a maximizer with $D\ge3$ is determined by four numbers $(c,s,a,b)$: $c$ cherries,
$a$ arms $A_s$ and $b$ arms $A_{s+1}$. For each $n$ there are only a few thousand such configurations,
plus the configurations with $D\le2$. Each value has an explicit integer numerator and denominator. For
each $n\le491$ the kernel compares every candidate against the table entry by a cross-multiplied
inequality of natural numbers.

At $n=21$ two non-isomorphic spiders tie ($C^{10}$ and $C^3A_3^2$); the table records $C^3A_3^2$. The
table also shows how varied small maximizers are. Below $n=46$ arms of every size from one to eight
appear, with up to eight cherries at the centre. From $n=46$ on every arm is an $A_4$, $A_5$ or $A_6$,
but a few cherries remain at the centre until $n=333$, and the $A_4$/$A_6$ choice keeps shifting until
$n=2319$.

\begin{inlean}
\lean{BGSpiderTable.lean} proves the soundness of the check (\lean{spider\_opt\_of\_check}); the table
itself is \lean{BGSpiderTableData.tabData}, and the 89 generated files \lean{BGSpiderTableChunk\_*} run
the check for about five sizes each, assembled by \lean{BGSpiderTableAll.spider\_opt\_table}. The table
rows are printed in \cref{app:table}, generated from the Lean file itself.
\end{inlean}

\subsection{Sizes from 492: structure, then 223 certificates}

For large $n$ a sweep is impossible, so we argue. Let $l$ be a spider maximizing $F$ with $n\ge492$. A
sequence of exchanges pins down its shape:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the centre has at least three children (otherwise a global bound shows it is far from optimal);
\item no arm $A_j$ has $j\ge13$: split it into $A_{j-11}$ and two $A_5$'s;
\item no arm has $7\le j\le12$: trade eleven $A_j$'s for $2j+1$ copies of $A_5$, and compare globally
  with the rule spider;
\item at most eight cherries: nine cherries lose to two $A_4$'s;
\item no arm has $j\le3$;
\item hence every arm is $A_4$, $A_5$ or $A_6$; by balance $A_4$ and $A_6$ never occur together; and there
  are at most ten of each.
\end{enumerate}
The global comparison uses the identity $(\prod T)^2=(3/2)^{n-1}\prod h$ with $h(\text{cherry})=1$ and
$h(A_j)=\tfrac23\alpha_j^2$; its tightest instance is $24.45<28.43$.

What remains is a finite list of shapes whose sizes grow with $n$. A candidate is determined by its
counts $(c,k_4,k_5,k_6)$, and
\[
  F=\Phi(c,k_4,k_5,k_6)=\Big(\frac32\Big)^{c}G_4^{k_4}G_5^{k_5}G_6^{k_6}
  \Big(1+\frac{c/3+k_4B_4+k_5B_5+k_6B_6}{c+k_4+k_5+k_6}\Big)
\]
with $G_4=\tfrac{513}{80}$, $G_5=\tfrac{621}{64}$, $G_6=\tfrac{6561}{448}$, $B_4=\tfrac3{19}$,
$B_5=\tfrac3{23}$, $B_6=\tfrac19$. The triple $(c,k_4,k_6)$ fixes the residue class $s$, hence the rule
winner's counts. After dividing out a common power of $G_5$, the comparison
$\Phi(\text{candidate})\le\Phi(\text{winner})$ becomes $P(m)\ge0$ for an explicit quadratic $P$ in the
common number $m$ of five-cherry arms. There are 189 triples and 223 certificates. 221 of them write
$P(L+t)$ with nonnegative coefficients in $t$, which is visibly positive for every $t\ge0$. The other
two, the competitions that decide the thresholds $722$ and $2319$, write it in the Bernstein basis on a
bounded interval, because there the rival really does win outside the interval (\cref{fig:race}).

\section{What exactly the Lean theorem says}\label{sec:lean}
%=====================================================================================================

The main theorem in Lean is
\begin{verbatim}
theorem R3Cert.BGMaximizerAll.bg_maximizer_all (n : Nat) (h4 : 4 <= n) :
    usize (bgMax n) = n /\ forall t : UTree,
      usize t = n -> Aobj t <= Aobj (bgMax n)
\end{verbatim}
and its companion \lean{bg\_maximizer\_all\_perm} states the same inequality for the permanent of the
Laplacian divided by the product of degrees, computed on the realized graphs. The proof of
\lean{bg\_maximizer\_all} is a ten-line case split on $n$ that calls the four range theorems. What a
reader must trust is not that proof (the kernel checked it) but the definitions it is about:

\begin{itemize}
\item \lean{UTree} is the type of finite rooted trees, built by \lean{node} from a list of subtrees, and
  \lean{usize} counts vertices. Every finite tree, rooted anywhere, is a \lean{UTree}; rerooting is
  handled inside the proof, so the quantifier really ranges over all trees.
\item \lean{Step3.realize (dtRealize t)} turns $t$ into an explicit edge list, and \lean{aGraph} turns
  that into a Mathlib \lean{SimpleGraph} on its vertex type.
\item \lean{lapl G} is the matrix with $\deg i$ on the diagonal, $-1$ on edges and $0$ elsewhere, and
  \lean{Matrix.permanent} is Mathlib's permanent.
\item \lean{Aobj t} is the weighted matching sum of the realized tree, and \lean{pi\_utree} proves that
  it equals \lean{(lapl (aGraph ...)).permanent} divided by the product of the degrees.
\item \lean{bgMax n} is \lean{spiderU (tab n)} for $n\le491$ and \lean{spiderU (W n)} otherwise, where
  \lean{tab} reads the table \lean{BGSpiderTableData.tabData} and \lean{W} implements the rule of
  \cref{thm:main}.
\end{itemize}

So \lean{bg\_maximizer\_all\_perm} states: for every $n\ge4$ and every tree $t$ on $n$ vertices,
$\per L/\prod\deg$ of $t$ is at most that of $\mathrm{bgMax}(n)$, with the permanent and the degrees read
off the actual graph. The file \lean{AxiomGuard.lean} prints the axioms of the 20 headline theorems. A
\lean{sorry} anywhere in their dependencies would appear there as \lean{sorryAx}, and a
\lean{native\_decide} as \lean{Lean.ofReduceBool}.

\paragraph{The same theorem in Mathlib's vocabulary.} Because \lean{UTree} and \lean{Aobj} are ours,
the repository also states the result using only Mathlib's definitions on the graph side, in the file
\texttt{formalization/Statement.lean}:
\begin{verbatim}
theorem brualdi_goldwasser_upper (n : Nat) (h4 : 4 <= n) {V : Type} [Fintype V]
    [DecidableEq V] (G : SimpleGraph V) [DecidableRel G.Adj] (hG : G.IsTree)
    (hV : Fintype.card V = n) :
    (G.lapMatrix R).permanent / (prod v, (G.degree v : R))
      <= (F (bgChildren n) : R)
\end{verbatim}
together with \lean{brualdi\_goldwasser\_attained}: some tree on \lean{Fin n} has ratio exactly
\lean{F (bgChildren n)}. Here \lean{IsTree}, \lean{lapMatrix} (degree matrix minus adjacency matrix),
\lean{Matrix.permanent} and \lean{degree} are Mathlib's, and the only definitions of ours a reader must
check are the closed form \lean{F} of \cref{sec:spiders} and the list of the centre's children
\lean{bgChildren n} (the table for $n\le491$, the rule for $n\ge492$). The bridge is three short
modules: \lean{TreePaths} proves that every Mathlib tree is isomorphic to the path graph of a
\lean{UTree} of the same size (by removing a leaf, using Mathlib's
\lean{IsTree.exists\_vert\_degree\_one\_of\_nontrivial}, and re-attaching it as a new last child);
\lean{TreeBridge} proves that this path graph is the realized graph; and \lean{BGStatement} proves that the
Laplacian ratio is invariant under graph isomorphism and assembles the two theorems.

\paragraph{What is not claimed in Lean.} The Lean theorem gives \emph{a} maximizer. That it is unique up
to isomorphism (except at $n=21$) is established by exact computation for $n\le491$ (\cref{sec:indep})
and for $n\ge2320$ by an exact rational certificate, but uniqueness is not formalized.

\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The byte-for-byte check matters for review. A reader does not have to read 57 files of packed integers;
it is enough to read the generator, run it, and see that it reproduces the files the kernel checked. And
even that is only needed to understand the proof, not to trust it: the kernel's verdict does not depend
on how the data was produced.

\paragraph{Making the kernel do it.} Three facts about the Lean kernel shaped the certificates.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item The kernel unfolds definitions by well-founded recursion very slowly, so every recursive check is
  structural, with an explicit fuel parameter.
\item It does not share the evaluation of repeated argument subterms: a loop state used twice is
  evaluated twice, and a chain of such loops blows up. We emit literal constants, and force evaluation of
  natural numbers before passing them on (\lean{BGEnvCert.Force}).
\item Arithmetic in $\mathbb{Q}$ is slow in the kernel, so large checks use natural numbers and fixed
  point, and only small ones use $\mathbb{Q}$.
\end{enumerate}
We use \lean{decide +kernel} and never \lean{native\_decide}, so the compiler is not trusted. Because the
kernel keeps every intermediate term of a declaration in memory, large checks are split into many small
theorems and many files, and a unifying file assembles them. Even so, the heaviest files need
up to about $63$\,GB each. The sharding is what makes the proof checkable on a single large workstation.

\section{Independent computations}\label{sec:indep}
%=====================================================================================================

None of the following is needed for the Lean proof. They are independent checks of the answer by
entirely different methods.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Certified search, $n\le491$.} A bottom-up search over all rooted trees, with no degree cap,
  storing every quantity as an outward-rounded interval and discarding a partial state only when it is
  provably strictly beaten in every completion. Surviving candidates are re-evaluated in exact rational
  arithmetic. For every $n\le491$ the exact maximum equals the table value, and \emph{every} maximizing
  tree is a spider. (952\,s on one core.)
\item \emph{Brute force.} Exact enumeration of all non-isomorphic trees agrees for $n\le18$.
\item \emph{A second kernel.} The repository contains a challenge/solution pair for the Lean FRO's
  Comparator~\cite{Comparator}: the challenge states \lean{brualdi\_goldwasser\_upper} and
  \lean{brualdi\_goldwasser\_attained} with \lean{sorry} and imports only the definitions of the answer.
  Comparator checks that the solution proves exactly those statements, using only the three standard
  axioms, and replays the exported proof both in Lean's kernel and in nanoda~\cite{nanoda}, an
  independent implementation of the Lean kernel in Rust.
\item \emph{Spider optimization.} An exhaustive exact optimization over the spider family for
  $n\le2400$, together with an exact rational certificate for $n\ge2320$, agrees with the table and the
  rule; an exact Pareto dynamic programme over the family with no balance assumption agrees for
  $n\le600$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%=====================================================================================================

\Cref{thm:main} answers the question Brualdi and Goldwasser asked. In this section we look at what the
answer says: about the shape of extremal trees, about the size of the maximum, about where the problem
sits among extremal problems on trees, and about permanents and methods more generally. We end with
what remains open. Unless stated otherwise, everything below is a consequence of \cref{thm:main} and the
closed form of \cref{sec:spiders}; the few statements that go beyond it are labelled as questions or
heuristics.

\subsection{The shape of the maximizer}

\paragraph{One hub, bounded depth.} Every maximizer is a spider, so every vertex is within distance
three of the centre and the diameter is at most $6$. (Among the maximizers in \cref{app:table} and those
given by the rule, the diameter is exactly $6$ once the centre carries two arms with cherries.) This is
a sharp contrast with the permanent itself: Brualdi and Goldwasser showed that $\per L(T)$ is largest for
the path, whose diameter is $n-1$. Dividing by $\prod_v\deg v$ does not merely rescale the problem; it
moves the extremal trees from maximal diameter to bounded diameter. At the other end, the minimum of
$\pi$ is attained by the star~\cite{BG1984}. So both extremes of the Laplacian ratio have a single hub:
the star, whose hub carries leaves, and the spider of five-cherry arms, whose hub carries second-level
hubs.

\paragraph{The degree profile.} For large $n$ the maximizer is made of $\lfloor(n-1)/11\rfloor-O(1)$
copies of the eleven-vertex block $A_5$, plus at most ten defect arms $A_4$ or $A_6$. Its degree sequence is
therefore, up to $O(1)$ vertices: about $5n/11$ leaves, about $5n/11$ vertices of degree $2$, about $n/11$
vertices of degree $6$, and a single vertex, the centre, of degree about $n/11$. Exactly one vertex has
unbounded degree. The degree-$6$ vertices are where the optimization happens: an arm vertex with $j$
cherries balances the number of cherries it collects against the degree it pays for them, and $j=5$ is
the unique best balance (\cref{fig:rates}).

\paragraph{Rigidity.} For large $n$ the maximizer is a star of identical blocks with a bounded number
of defects, and the defects are dictated by $n\bmod11$. This is the typical shape of answers in ergodic
optimization, where an optimal orbit is periodic and a subaction certifies it~\cite{Bousch2001,Jenkinson2019},
and indeed that is how the growth-rate proof works: the potential $\varrho_{\mathrm{wit}}$ is tight exactly
on the three vertex types of the optimal block (\cref{sec:ceiling}).

\paragraph{Why the problem resisted guesses.} The earlier conjectures were natural extrapolations from
small trees, and \cref{app:table} shows why that is treacherous. For small $n$ the maximizer wanders
through arms of many sizes, with many cherries at the centre. It settles into $A_4$'s, $A_5$'s and
$A_6$'s only at $n=46$, keeps a few centre cherries until $n=333$, and the choice between $A_4$'s and
$A_6$'s keeps changing until $n=2319$. The Wu--Dong--Lai formulas are exactly right up to $n=22$ (and for
even $n$ up to $34$), so the conjecture was well supported by the data one could reasonably compute by
hand; it simply described a regime that ends early.

\subsection{The size of the maximum}

\Cref{thm:growth} pins the growth rate but leaves a factor of about $16$ between its lower and upper
constants. \Cref{thm:main} removes it: the maximum is known exactly for every $n$, and its normalized
value converges along each residue class.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:constant}
Let $\rho=(621/64)^{1/11}$, and put
\[
  \delta_4=\frac{T(A_4)}{\rho^{9}}=\frac{513/80}{\rho^{9}}\approx0.9989741,\qquad
  \delta_6=\frac{T(A_6)}{\rho^{13}}=\frac{6561/448}{\rho^{13}}\approx0.9984859 .
\]
\begin{enumerate}
\item For every $n\equiv1\pmod{11}$ with $n\ge254$,
\[
  \max_{|T|=n}\pi(T)=\frac{26}{23}\Big(\frac{621}{64}\Big)^{(n-1)/11}.
\]
\item For each residue $s=6(n-1)\bmod11$, as $n\to\infty$ within the class,
\[
  \frac{\max_{|T|=n}\pi(T)}{\rho^{\,n-1}}\ \longrightarrow\ c_s=\frac{26}{23}\,\delta_4^{\,k_4(s)}\,\delta_6^{\,k_6(s)},
\]
where $k_6(s)=s$, $k_4(s)=0$ for $1\le s\le4$; $k_4(s)=11-s$, $k_6(s)=0$ for $5\le s\le10$; and
$k_4(0)=k_6(0)=0$. In particular
\[
  \limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\max\pi}{\rho^{n-1}}=\frac{26}{23}\approx1.130435,\qquad
  \liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\max\pi}{\rho^{n-1}}=c_5=\frac{26}{23}\delta_4^{\,6}\approx1.123494 .
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For $n\equiv1\pmod{11}$ we have $s=0$, and for $254\le n\le491$ the table, and for $n\ge492$ the rule,
gives the spider $A_5^{k}$ with $k=(n-1)/11$. Its value is $T(A_5)^k(1+y(A_5))=(621/64)^k\cdot\tfrac{26}{23}$,
since every arm sends the message $\tfrac3{23}$. For general $s$ and $n\ge2320$ the rule gives $k_4$ arms
$A_4$, $k_6$ arms $A_6$ and $k_5$ arms $A_5$ with $n-1=9k_4+11k_5+13k_6$, so
\[
  \frac{F}{\rho^{\,n-1}}=\delta_4^{k_4}\,\delta_6^{k_6}\Big(1+\frac{k_4\cdot\frac3{19}+k_5\cdot\frac3{23}+k_6\cdot\frac19}{k_4+k_5+k_6}\Big),
\]
and the bracket tends to $1+\tfrac3{23}=\tfrac{26}{23}$ because $k_4,k_6$ are bounded while $k_5\to\infty$.
\end{proof}

So the maximum is $c_{s(n)}\rho^{\,n-1}(1+O(1/n))$, with eleven explicit constants, all within $0.7\%$ of
each other. The period-$11$ oscillation in \cref{fig:ratio} is exactly this: each class carries a fixed
number of defect arms, and each defect costs a factor $\delta_4$ or $\delta_6$, about one part in a
thousand. The first part of the corollary is also a pleasant closed form: for $n\equiv1\pmod{11}$ the
answer to Brualdi and Goldwasser's question is a single power of a rational number, times $\tfrac{26}{23}$.
(The corollary is not stated separately in Lean, but it is a finite computation from the formal
theorem and the closed form $F$.)

\paragraph{Where the late switches come from.} The two switches in the rule, at $n=722$ and $n=2319$
(\cref{fig:race}), have a simple explanation. In a class $s\in\{1,2,3,4\}$ the remainder can be absorbed
either by $s$ arms $A_6$ or by $11-s$ arms $A_4$. In the bulk, the sixes are cheaper: the fours cost
$(11-s)\log(1/\delta_4)$ and the sixes cost $s\log(1/\delta_6)$, and for $s\le4$ the former is larger. But
fours send larger messages to the centre ($\tfrac3{19}>\tfrac3{23}>\tfrac19$), and the root bonus
$\log(1+R/D)$ rewards that by an amount proportional to $1/D$, where $D\approx n/11$ is the centre degree.
So the fours win while $D$ is small and lose once the bonus has decayed below the bulk difference. Equating
the two to first order predicts switches near $n\approx738$ for $s=3$ and $n\approx2335$ for $s=4$, against
the true switches at $722\mid733$ and $2319\mid2330$; for $s=2$ it predicts about $449$, against the true
switch at $423\mid434$ (below $492$, where the table takes over). The heuristic explains the thresholds;
the certificates of \cref{sec:spiders} are what prove them.

\subsection{Where the problem sits among extremal problems on trees}

Many classical tree invariants have the path and the star as their extremal trees. The number of
matchings (the Hosoya index), for example, is largest for the path and smallest for the
star~\cite{WagnerGutman2010}. Csikvári's generalized tree shift~\cite{Csikvari2010} explains a great deal of
this: it is a partial order on trees with the path at the bottom and the star at the top, and many
invariants (the number of closed walks, the spectral radius, the Estrada index and others) are monotone
along it, so their extremes are forced to be the path and the star.

The Laplacian ratio is a weighted count of matchings, but it is not of this kind. Its minimum is the
star, the top of the poset; if it were monotone along the poset its maximum would be the path, and it is
not. The weight $1/(\deg u\deg v)$ on each matched edge is what breaks monotonicity: it rewards matchings
near low-degree vertices, but low degree limits how many edges a vertex can contribute. The result is an
extremal tree that is neither a path nor a star, and whose shape depends on $n$ modulo $11$. This suggests
a natural family of problems in between.

\begin{itemize}
\item For $\alpha\ge0$ let $\pi_\alpha(T)=\sum_M\prod_{uv\in M}(\deg u\deg v)^{-\alpha}$. Then $\pi_0$ is
  the Hosoya index, maximized by the path, and $\pi_1=\pi$ is maximized by spiders of five-cherry arms.
  How does the maximizer move as $\alpha$ increases from $0$ to $1$ and beyond: through which shapes, and
  at which values of $\alpha$ does it change? The cavity recursion and the subaction method apply
  verbatim, with $\log\rho$ replaced by an $\alpha$-dependent rate.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{A statistical-physics reading}

By \cref{thm:matchsum}, $\pi(T)$ is the partition function of a monomer-dimer system on $T$ in which a
dimer on the edge $uv$ has activity $1/(\deg u\deg v)$~\cite{HL1972}. In that language, $\log\rho$ is the
largest free energy per vertex that any tree can achieve, and \cref{thm:sharp} says the five-cherry arm is
its unique ``ground state'': the only pendant structure that achieves it exactly. The maximizer is then a
ground-state configuration with the fewest defects that the vertex count allows, and $\log(1/\delta_4)$ and
$\log(1/\delta_6)$ are the energies of the two cheapest defects. The subaction $\varrho_{\mathrm{wit}}$ of
\cref{sec:ceiling} plays the role that calibrated sub-actions play in Aubry--Mather theory and ergodic
optimization~\cite{Bousch2001,Jenkinson2019}: a local certificate that no configuration can beat the
ground state. It would be interesting to know how general this is. For which other weighted matching
problems on trees, or other tree functionals computed by a cavity recursion, is the extremal tree a
periodic arrangement of a single block, certified by a finite piecewise-affine subaction?

\subsection{Permanents}

The permanent is \#P-hard~\cite{Valiant1979}, and its extremal theory is thin: questions of the form
``which matrix in a natural class maximizes the permanent?'' are often hard even when the class is
simple. For Laplacians of trees, the matching-sum formula turns the permanent into a partition function
that factorizes over the tree, and that is what made this problem solvable. The same structure applies
to other matrix functions of trees built from $D$ and $A$. For example, the signless Laplacian $D+A$ of a
tree has the same permanent, since a matched edge contributes $A_{ij}A_{ji}=1$ either way, so
\cref{thm:main} answers the analogous question for it too. Immanants of $L$ interpolate between the
determinant and the permanent, and orderings of trees by such quantities have been
studied~\cite{Csikvari2010}.

For graphs with cycles the matching-sum formula no longer holds: a permutation can follow a cycle of the
graph, and such terms contribute alongside the matchings. Brualdi and Goldwasser also studied bipartite
graphs~\cite{BG1984}, and the maximum of $\per L/\prod\deg$ over connected graphs, or over connected
bipartite graphs, on $n$ vertices is a natural next question. Whether cycles help or hurt, and whether an
analogue of the sharp ceiling holds, we do not know.

\subsection{Method}

Most of the proof is a small number of ideas (the cavity recursion, the subaction, tangent envelopes,
exchange arguments) applied through a large number of verified inequalities. This is a common shape for
extremal results, and such proofs are often hard to check: the ideas are in the paper, but the
inequalities are in a computer program that a referee cannot easily audit. Here every inequality is
checked by the Lean kernel, and every certificate can be regenerated and compared byte for byte. We think
this division, conceptual proof for people and certificate checking for the kernel, is a good model for
computer-assisted extremal combinatorics, and the practical lessons of \cref{sec:telperion} (sharding,
fixed point, structural recursion) apply well beyond this problem. The method also degrades gracefully:
where a uniform argument was too weak (small $n$), it was replaced by a larger certificate rather than
by an unchecked computation.

A related point concerns how the work was done. Much of the exploration, formalization and certificate
engineering was carried out with an AI system. In a project of that kind the natural worry is an
error that no one notices. Formal verification changes that: an error in a certificate or a proof step
is a failed build, not a hidden mistake. The remaining human responsibility, checking that the
definitions and the statement mean what the paper says, is exactly the part that \cref{sec:lean} isolates.

\subsection{What remains}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{A conceptual proof of the reduction.} The thresholds $150$, $491$ and $492$ are artefacts of
  the method. A direct argument that every maximizer is a spider, for all $n$, would be more
  illuminating than the certificates, and would probably say something about when cavity-recursion
  optimizers are ``star-shaped''.
\item \emph{Uniqueness in Lean.} Uniqueness (except at $n=21$) is known by computation and certificate
  but not formalized.
\item \emph{The interpolating family $\pi_\alpha$.} Which trees maximize $\pi_\alpha$, and where does the
  maximizer change shape?
\item \emph{Constrained versions.} Trees of maximum degree at most $\Delta$ (the rate cells of
  \cref{sec:reduction} already work under degree caps), trees with a prescribed degree sequence, and trees
  with a given diameter, the constraint under which the minimum was studied~\cite{Wu2024}.
\item \emph{Graphs with cycles.} Bipartite and general connected graphs, as above.
\item \emph{Independent review.} The kernel checks the deductions; human review of the statement and the
  definitions is still needed, and welcome.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item The result has not been refereed. The Lean kernel checks the deductions; the correspondence
  between the formal definitions and the mathematical statement (\cref{sec:lean}) is what a human
  reviewer should check.
\item The proof is long and mostly computational. We do not have a short conceptual proof that the
  maximizer is a spider, only per-vertex inequalities verified by certificate.
\item Uniqueness is not formalized.
\item The trusted base is the Lean kernel, Mathlib's definitions of the permanent and of simple graphs,
  and our definitions listed in \cref{sec:lean}.
\item The full build needs a machine with about $96$\,GB of memory (the heaviest certificate file alone
  peaks at $63$\,GB) and takes one to two hours on a recent workstation.
\end{itemize}
