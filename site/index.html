Which tree maximizes the Laplacian ratio?

In 1984 Brualdi and Goldwasser asked which tree on n vertices maximizes π(T) = per L(T) / ∏ deg(v). This project answers the question for every n, with a proof checked by the Lean kernel.

The certificates behind the proof were produced with Telperion. See it at work in the Telperion Registry Explorer, which puts this theorem first.

A cherry is a pendant path of length two. An arm Aj is a vertex, hanging from a centre, that carries j cherries. A spider of cherry arms is a centre whose branches are all leaves, cherries or arms.

Theorem. For every n ≥ 4 the Laplacian ratio is maximized by a spider of cherry arms. For n ≥ 492 the centre carries only arms, almost all A5. With s = 6(n−1) mod 11:
  • s = 1–4: s arms are A6 (but for s = 3, n ≤ 722 and s = 4, n ≤ 2319, instead 11−s arms are A4);
  • s = 5–10: 11−s arms are A4.
For 4 ≤ n ≤ 491 the maximizer is an explicit table (see Explore).

Why five cherries

The weight of a pendant subtree is a matching sum, and the five-cherry arm A5 (11 vertices) has weight exactly 621/64. We prove that every pendant subtree b has weight at most ρ|b| with ρ = (621/64)1/11 ≈ 1.22948, and that A5 is the only one attaining it. So the maximum grows exactly like ρn:

(64/621) ρn ≤ max|T|=n π(T) ≤ 2 ρn−1

A maximizer uses as many five-cherry arms as the arithmetic of n allows. The residue n−1 mod 11 is absorbed by a few A4 (9 vertices) or A6 (13 vertices), and which of those wins is a delicate second-order competition, which is why the thresholds 722 and 2319 appear.

Why five, rather than four or six? Give every matched edge an extra weight λ: as λ grows, the best arm climbs 3, 4, 5, 6, … and runs off to infinity at 1 + √5, where the golden ratio appears. Brualdi and Goldwasser's λ = 1 sits on the five-cherry rung. See Why five: the ladder. Along the whole ladder, for every λ > 0, the best block is now known to set the growth rate of πλ: a computer-assisted result (interval arithmetic) up to 1 + √5, extended past it by a short hand argument described on that tab. That cherry regime, λ ≥ 1 + √5, is now also kernel-checked in Lean (formalization/cherry): the ceiling with equality only for the cherry, a strict upper bound for every tree, the lower bound, and the growth rate √(1 + λ/2). For every λ > 0 Lean also checks the one-block formula, that the best branch sets the growth rate, and the witness framework of the computer-assisted proof. Below 1 + √5 it reduces the result to two external inputs (witnesses and a best arm on [0.1, 1 + √5); the branch bound itself and a best arm on (0, 0.1]) that it does not prove: that range still rests on computer-assisted interval arithmetic.

The maximum divided by ρn−1, for 4 ≤ n ≤ 1200. It stays between the proven bounds, and oscillates with period 11 once the arms settle.

History

1984: the question. Brualdi and Goldwasser studied the permanent of the Laplacian of trees and bipartite graphs. Over trees on n vertices, the permanent itself is smallest for the star and largest for the path, which mostly rewards having many vertices of degree two. Dividing by the product of the degrees removes that effect, and they introduced the ratio π(T) for that purpose. They proved π(T) ≥ 2, with equality exactly for the star, and posed extremal problems for the ratio, among them the maximum over all trees on n vertices.

2024–2025: the minimum side, and a conjecture. Wu, Dong, Lai and Zeng determined the minimum over trees of diameter at least k (2024), and Wu, Dong and Lai the minimum over trees with a given bipartition (2025). For the maximum, Wu, Dong and Lai conjectured explicit subdivided stars. For odd n: a centre carrying only cherries, with π = 2(3/2)(n−3)/2. For even n: explicit variants in the classes n ≡ 0 and 2 mod 4. Cherries are the best small building block, and for small n the conjecture is right.

2026: counterexamples. P. Pant refuted the conjecture with caterpillars T(a1,…,am): a path whose i-th vertex carries ai cherries. His three families T(3,t,3), T(t,t,t,t) and T(t,t,t+1,t) beat the conjecture from n = 23, 36 and 38, one in each residue class, and he noticed that at n = 21 the tree T(3,3,3) ties the conjectured value. He concluded that the maximum remains open.

A caterpillar with three hubs is a spider seen from its middle hub, which is why Pant's T(3,4,3) can be optimal. With four or more hubs it is never a spider, so by the reduction theorem it is never optimal. Wu, Dong and Lai were right that cherries are the basic unit. Pant was right that grouping cherries on several hubs beats a single hub of cherries. The eventual answer is one hub carrying arms, each a second-level hub with exactly five cherries.

A spider is fixed by how many cherries sit at the centre and how many arms of each size it has. Its ratio has a closed form, F = (3/2)c + Σj ∏ αj · (1 + (c/3 + Σ bj)/D), so you can explore the whole family by hand. Change the counts, or scramble a spider and let the balance lemma repair it: whenever the centre has at least three children, moving one cherry from an arm to an arm at least two smaller strictly increases F.

Why five cherries, and not four or six? Put the problem in a family. Give every matched edge an extra weight λ > 0, so that πλ(T) = ΣM λ|M| ∏uv∈M 1/(deg u deg v). Brualdi and Goldwasser's ratio is λ = 1. For each λ, ask which building block (a leaf, a cherry, or an arm Aj carrying j cherries) gives the most weight per vertex.

The arm ladder. As λ increases, the best of these blocks (leaf, cherry or arm) climbs A3, A4, A5, A6, …: each neighbouring pair changes places exactly once, at breakpoints λ3 ≈ 0.4305, λ4 ≈ 0.8725, λ5 ≈ 1.1924, λ6 ≈ 1.4356, …, which increase to 1 + √5 ≈ 3.236. There the arms run off to infinity and plain cherries take over. The Brualdi–Goldwasser value λ = 1 sits on the A5 rung. That is why the answer is five.

Weight per vertex of each block, relative to the cherry: (log T(Aj))/(2j+1) − ½ log((2+λ)/2), for j = 1, …, 12. Above zero the arm beats a plain cherry. The highlighted curve is the best of these blocks at the chosen λ.

One balance condition, one cubic

A long arm is j cherries plus one hub vertex. The hub is worth adding only if what it buys beats what that same vertex would earn inside a cherry. As j grows the hub multiplies the weight by H(λ) = 2(1+λ)/(2+λ), and a cherry earns ζ(λ) = √((2+λ)/2) per vertex. So long arms win exactly when H > ζ. Squaring and clearing denominators, the balance point is

8(1+λ)2 = (2+λ)3  ⇔  (x−2)(x2−6x+4) = 0   with x = 2+λ,   so λ = 0 or λ = 1 ± √5.

At the top of the ladder, λ = 1 + √5, the hub factor and the cherry rate coincide, and both equal the golden ratio φ = (1+√5)/2. The other root, λ = 0, is the bottom of the ladder. There, to first order in λ, the matching sum only sees single edges, and the problem becomes maximizing the Randić index Σuv 1/(deg u deg v), whose maximum over large trees, (15n−1)/56, is attained by spiders of three-cherry arms A3 when n ≡ 1 (mod 7): the bottom rung of the ladder. So one cubic governs the whole ladder, with the Randić index at one end and the golden ratio at the other.

The hub factor H(λ) against the cherry rate ζ(λ). They cross once for λ > 0, at 1 + √5, at height φ.

The ceiling along the ladder: every λ > 0

The ladder says which block is best. A second question is whether that block also sets the growth rate of πλ: whether no pendant subtree of any shape beats the best block per vertex. At λ = 1 that is the sharp ceiling of the Lean proof. It is now proved for every λ > 0, with no gaps: by computer-assisted interval arithmetic (mpmath interval arithmetic, independently re-implemented in Arb) up to 1 + √5, and by a short hand argument from there on. On the whole range the maximal growth rate of πλ is the best-atom rate f*(λ), so the ladder picture above, which arm is best, is the whole story for the growth rate. The proof runs in six regimes:

  • 0 < λ ≤ 0.1: a degree-typed induction. The leaf, the cherry and the arms are exact types, and every other branch carries a bound proportional to λ. The interval boxes include λ = 0 itself, and the only equality is the three-cherry arm A3. Computer-assisted (interval arithmetic, independently re-implemented in Arb), not formalized in Lean.
  • 0.1 ≤ λ ≤ 0.47: a power shoulder.
  • 0.47 ≤ λ ≤ 1.6: a quadratic shoulder. This is the regime that contains λ = 1.
  • 1.6 ≤ λ ≤ 3.22: a three-piece linear certificate.
  • 3.22 ≤ λ < 1 + √5: a hand argument for the thin window in which the best arm runs off to infinity, with about 15/(1 + √5 − λ) cherries (that constant is observed, not proved).
  • λ ≥ 1 + √5: the cherry regime. Here the cherry is the best atom and the growth rate is ½ log(1 + λ/2). A short hand argument shows that the expected number of unmatched vertices in the weighted matching model is at least 2n/(2 + λ) once λ ≥ 2, which makes Tb(λ)/(2 + λ)n/2 nonincreasing in λ; anchored at the single point 1 + √5, where the ceiling is verified by calculus using the golden-ratio identities, that gives the ceiling for every larger λ. This regime is also kernel-checked in Lean (formalization/cherry): the ceiling with equality only for the cherry, the bounds on the maximum, and the growth rate.

For 0 < λ < 1 + √5 this result is computer-assisted (interval arithmetic). Lean checks the witness framework of this proof for every λ > 0 and reduces the range below 1 + √5 to two external inputs (witnesses and a best arm on [0.1, 1 + √5); the branch bound itself and a best arm on (0, 0.1]), which it does not prove, so that range still rests on computer-assisted interval arithmetic. For λ ≥ 1 + √5 it is a hand proof (calculus at 1 + √5 with the golden-ratio identities, then a monotonicity argument), and it is also kernel-checked in Lean (formalization/cherry), by a different route at the anchor. At λ = 1 exactly it is also the sharp ceiling of the Lean proof. For every λ > 0 Lean also checks the one-block formula: with ρ(λ) the supremum of Tb1/|b| over all branches, the maximum lies between Tb⌊(n−1)/|b|⌋ and (1+λ)ρn−1, and its n-th root tends to ρ. Below 1 + √5 the one-block formula alone does not determine ρ(λ); its value there comes from the computer-assisted inputs. It is about the growth rate only: it says nothing about the shape of the maximizers for λ ≠ 1. As λ → 0 the rate is governed to first order by the Randić limit above, which is why the bottom regime is anchored at A3.

What is proved and what is not. The ladder (which block is best among leaves, cherries and arms) and the balance cubic are elementary and proved. At λ = 1, no pendant subtree of any shape beats A5; that is the sharp ceiling in the Lean proof. For every λ > 0 the same ceiling holds: for 0 < λ < 1 + √5 by the computer-assisted (interval arithmetic) arguments above, which Lean reduces to their numerical inputs but does not prove, and for λ ≥ 1 + √5 by a hand proof (calculus at the anchor 1 + √5, then a monotonicity argument) that is also kernel-checked in Lean. That the maximizers for other λ are spiders of the best arm is supported by exact computation and is being prepared for publication; treat it as a conjecture for now.

The cherry regime, λ ≥ 1 + √5

Past the top of the ladder the cherry is the best block, and the claim is that no pendant subtree of any shape beats it per vertex: every planted branch b has Tb(λ) ≤ (1 + λ/2)|b|/2, with equality only for the cherry. Here Tb is the weight of the branch, computed by the cavity recursion: a leaf has T = 1 and message y = 1; a vertex with children c has d = #children + 1, R = Σ yc, Tb = ∏ Tc · (d + λR)/d and yb = 1/(d + λR). The plots below compute it in your browser.

Per-vertex rate of the arms Aj (j cherries on a hub, 2j+1 vertices), fj = log T(Aj)/(2j+1) with T(Aj) = cj(1 + tj/(j+1)), c = 1 + λ/2, t = λ/(2+λ), minus the cherry's rate ½ log(1 + λ/2), for j = 1, …, 12. Every arm is below zero from 1 + √5 on: the cherry is the unique best block.

For each branch, (1/|b|) log(Tb(λ)/(1 + λ/2)|b|/2), which is (1/|b|) log(Tb(λ)/(2+λ)|b|/2) + ½ log 2: the normalized ratio Tb/(2+λ)|b|/2, per vertex and shifted so that the ceiling is the zero line. Every curve is nonincreasing on [2, ∞), and the cherry's is flat at zero. Below 1 + √5 a branch can still sit above zero (A5 does at λ = 2); from the anchor on, none can.

The anchor at λ = 1 + √5, where the ceiling reads Tb ≤ φ|b|, is proved by one-variable calculus using the golden identities 1 + λ/2 = φ2 and 1 + λ = φ3. The extension to every larger λ is the monotonicity drawn above. For λ ≥ 1 + √5 the ceiling, its equality case, the bounds on Mn(λ) below and the growth rate are also kernel-checked in Lean (formalization/cherry); the Lean proof of the anchor takes a different route.

The upper bound is never attained

Let Mn(λ) be the largest πλ over trees on n vertices. In the cherry regime the ceiling gives (1 + λ/2)⌊(n−1)/2⌋ ≤ Mn(λ) < (1 + λ)(1 + λ/2)(n−1)/2 for every n ≥ 1, and the upper bound is strict: no tree reaches it. The lower bound comes from a center carrying ⌊(n−1)/2⌋ cherries (and one leaf when n is even). Here Mn is computed exactly as stated, by running the cavity recursion on every tree with n ≤ 12 vertices (987 trees, listed when the page is built); for larger n only the bounds are drawn.

The one-block formula, every λ > 0

For every λ > 0 the growth rate of Mn(λ) is set by single blocks. Let ρ(λ) be the supremum of Tb1/|b| over all finite planted branches b, the leaf included. Then for every branch b and every n ≥ 1

Tb⌊(n−1)/|b|⌋ ≤ Mn(λ) ≤ (1+λ) ρ(λ)n−1,
ρ(λ) ≤ 1 + λ,   lim Mn1/n = ρ(λ).

The lower bound is the tree drawn below: a center carrying k = ⌊(n−1)/|b|⌋ copies of b and the remaining n−1−k|b| vertices as leaves. Pick a branch and an activity.

What is checked where. The one-block formula above (the definition of ρ as a supremum, the two bounds, ρ ≤ 1 + λ and the limit) is kernel-checked in Lean for every λ > 0 (formalization/cherry), and so is ρ(λ) = √(1 + λ/2) for λ ≥ 1 + √5. Below 1 + √5, that ρ(λ) = ef*(λ), the best rate among the leaf, the cherry and the arms, is proved by computer-assisted interval arithmetic: Lean derives it from two external inputs (witnesses and a best arm on [0.1, 1 + √5); the branch bound itself and a best arm on (0, 0.1]) but does not prove those inputs. The curve ρ drawn here uses that value, computed over the cherry and the arms Aj with j ≤ 400 (near 1 + √5 the best arm grows without bound, so the curve is a close lower approximation there). At λ = 1 the arm A5 attains ρ(1) = (621/64)1/11.

For large n almost every arm is A5, and the leftover n−1 mod 11 is absorbed in one of two ways: s arms A6 (13 vertices each) or 11−s arms A4 (9 vertices each), where s = 6(n−1) mod 11. Fours send bigger messages to the centre; sixes waste less weight. Which wins depends on n, and in two classes the answer flips late: at n = 722 and at n = 2319. Pick a class and slide n; the sign is decided in exact arithmetic.

The curve is 106 × (Ffours/Fsixes − 1): above zero the fours win. In the proof, these two late crossings are the only competitions that need Bernstein-basis certificates; the other 221 comparisons are one-sided for every n ≥ 492.

The proof has three layers: a sharp inequality that fixes the growth rate, a reduction showing every maximizer is a spider, and an exact optimization over spiders. Each is split by size.

1

Matching sums and the cavity recursion

For a tree, per L(T) = ΣM ∏v unmatched deg v over matchings M, so π(T) = ΣM ∏uv∈M 1/(deg u deg v). A pendant subtree b with root degree d has weight Tb = ∏ Tc (d+R)/d and message yb = 1/(d+R), where R = Σ yc over the children.

2

The sharp ceiling

Tb ≤ ρ|b| for every pendant subtree, with equality only at A5. Proof: log Tb − |b| log ρ is a sum of per-vertex terms, and an explicit subaction (a potential on vertex states) makes every per-vertex inequality hold, so the sum telescopes. The only tight constraints are the three vertex types of A5, consistent because 27 · 23 = 621.

Lean: R3Cert.BGSCL.bg_sharp, R3Cert.Step3.bg_max_growth

3

Every maximizer is a spider

Root at a vertex of maximum degree k. A non-atom child must cost more than it can gain.

  • k ≥ 24: a tangent price makes A5 the envelope; non-atoms lose at least 1/75.
  • k ≤ 23, n ≥ 492: 32 rate cells give a linear deficit α|b| for every non-atom branch.
  • 150 ≤ n ≤ 491: rate cells with degree-dependent credits and per-(k, n) root knapsacks.
  • 7 ≤ n ≤ 149: a sharded Bellman envelope certificate (57 Lean files), fixed point, kernel-evaluated.
  • 4 ≤ n ≤ 6: every tree evaluated exactly.
4

The best spider

A spider's value has a closed form. An exact balance identity shows arm sizes in a maximizer differ by at most one. For n ≤ 491 the kernel sweeps every balanced configuration (89 chunk files). For n ≥ 492 exchange arguments confine the maximizer to arms of 4, 5, 6 cherries, and 223 polynomial certificates (checked by ring and norm_num) pick out the rule.

Read the paper (PDF) for the full argument.

The statement

theorem R3Cert.BGMaximizerAll.bg_maximizer_all_perm (n : Nat) (h4 : 4 <= n)
    (t : UTree) (ht : usize t = n) :
    (lapl (aGraph (realize (dtRealize t)))).permanent
        / (prod v, (aGraph (realize (dtRealize t))).degree v)
      <= (lapl (aGraph (realize (dtRealize (bgMax n))))).permanent
        / (prod v, (aGraph (realize (dtRealize (bgMax n)))).degree v)

Here lapl G has deg on the diagonal and −1 on edges, and permanent is Mathlib's. What a reviewer should check is that these definitions mean what they say; the kernel checks the rest.

Status and limits

  • Not yet refereed. We welcome review and issues.
  • Uniqueness of the maximizer is established computationally, not in Lean.
  • The growth-rate ceiling for the λ-family for every λ > 0 (see Why five: the ladder) is computer-assisted (interval arithmetic) for 0 < λ < 1 + √5 and a hand proof (calculus at 1 + √5 with the golden-ratio identities, then a monotonicity argument) for λ ≥ 1 + √5. The cherry regime λ ≥ 1 + √5 is also kernel-checked in a separate Lean project (formalization/cherry: the branch ceiling with its equality case, the bounds on the maximum, ρ(λ) and the growth rate), together with the one-block formula and the witness framework for every λ > 0 (the witness theorem (a)–(c), and (d) up to one step taken as a hypothesis). Below 1 + √5 Lean only reduces the ceiling to two external inputs, witnesses and a best arm on [0.1, 1 + √5) and the branch bound itself and a best arm on (0, 0.1], which it does not prove; that range still rests on computer-assisted interval arithmetic, and its equality clause, the explicit witnesses and the typed induction are not formalized. That project was audited five times by independent AI-run audits and replayed by Comparator with nanoda (for the witness framework, (a)–(c) only; run locally, not sandboxed). The Lean theorem about the maximizer itself is λ = 1 only.
