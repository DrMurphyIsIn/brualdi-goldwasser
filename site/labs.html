A spider is fixed by how many cherries sit at the centre and how many arms of each size it has. Its ratio has a closed form, F = (3/2)c + Σj ∏ αj · (1 + (c/3 + Σ bj)/D), so you can explore the whole family by hand. Change the counts, or scramble a spider and let the balance lemma repair it: whenever the centre has at least three children, moving one cherry from an arm to an arm at least two smaller strictly increases F.

Why five cherries, and not four or six? Put the problem in a family. Give every matched edge an extra weight λ > 0, so that πλ(T) = ΣM λ|M| ∏uv∈M 1/(deg u deg v). Brualdi and Goldwasser's ratio is λ = 1. For each λ, ask which building block (a leaf, a cherry, or an arm Aj carrying j cherries) gives the most weight per vertex.

The arm ladder. As λ increases, the best of these blocks (leaf, cherry or arm) climbs A3, A4, A5, A6, …: each neighbouring pair changes places exactly once, at breakpoints λ3 ≈ 0.4305, λ4 ≈ 0.8725, λ5 ≈ 1.1924, λ6 ≈ 1.4356, …, which increase to 1 + √5 ≈ 3.236. There the arms run off to infinity and plain cherries take over. The Brualdi–Goldwasser value λ = 1 sits on the A5 rung. That is why the answer is five.

Weight per vertex of each block, relative to the cherry: (log T(Aj))/(2j+1) − ½ log((2+λ)/2), for j = 1, …, 12. Above zero the arm beats a plain cherry. The highlighted curve is the best of these blocks at the chosen λ.

One balance condition, one cubic

A long arm is j cherries plus one hub vertex. The hub is worth adding only if what it buys beats what that same vertex would earn inside a cherry. As j grows the hub multiplies the weight by H(λ) = 2(1+λ)/(2+λ), and a cherry earns ζ(λ) = √((2+λ)/2) per vertex. So long arms win exactly when H > ζ. Squaring and clearing denominators, the balance point is

8(1+λ)2 = (2+λ)3  ⇔  (x−2)(x2−6x+4) = 0   with x = 2+λ,   so λ = 0 or λ = 1 ± √5.

At the top of the ladder, λ = 1 + √5, the hub factor and the cherry rate coincide, and both equal the golden ratio φ = (1+√5)/2. The other root, λ = 0, is the bottom of the ladder. There, to first order in λ, the matching sum only sees single edges, and the problem becomes maximizing the Randić index Σuv 1/(deg u deg v), whose maximum over large trees, (15n−1)/56, is attained by spiders of three-cherry arms A3 when n ≡ 1 (mod 7): the bottom rung of the ladder. So one cubic governs the whole ladder, with the Randić index at one end and the golden ratio at the other.

The hub factor H(λ) against the cherry rate ζ(λ). They cross once for λ > 0, at 1 + √5, at height φ.

What is proved and what is not. The ladder (which block is best among leaves, cherries and arms) and the balance cubic are elementary and proved. At λ = 1, no pendant subtree of any shape beats A5; that is the sharp ceiling in the Lean proof. For other λ, that the best block is also the best pendant subtree of any shape (so that it sets the growth rate of πλ), and that the maximizers are spiders of the best arm, is supported by exact computation and is being prepared for publication; treat it as a conjecture for now.

For large n almost every arm is A5, and the leftover n−1 mod 11 is absorbed in one of two ways: s arms A6 (13 vertices each) or 11−s arms A4 (9 vertices each), where s = 6(n−1) mod 11. Fours send bigger messages to the centre; sixes waste less weight. Which wins depends on n, and in two classes the answer flips late: at n = 722 and at n = 2319. Pick a class and slide n; the sign is decided in exact arithmetic.

The curve is 106 × (Ffours/Fsixes − 1): above zero the fours win. In the proof, these two late crossings are the only competitions that need Bernstein-basis certificates; the other 221 comparisons are one-sided for every n ≥ 492.
